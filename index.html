01 · Start with the basics

What is HIV?

HIV stands for Human Immunodeficiency Virus. It is a virus that attacks the body's immune system, the part of the body that fights off illness.

HIV is one of the most misunderstood viruses. Myths and rumours cause unnecessary panic and discrimination against people living with it. Spreading accurate knowledge is the best way to end that.

HIV is among us. Many people in our community are living healthy, long lives with the virus. More awareness is still needed, both to reduce new infections and to reduce the stigma people face.

♡

People are more than a diagnosis

HIV status does not define someone's worth, relationships, ambitions or future.

✚

Care makes a difference

Testing, effective treatment and support help people protect their health and live well.

02 · Our shared history

Where did HIV come from?

Scientists have traced HIV back to animals. It is a zoonosis, meaning an infection that began in animals and later passed to people. Its ancestor is SIV (simian immunodeficiency virus), a virus that infects monkeys and apes in Africa.

There are two main types of HIV, and each came from a different animal:

🌍

HIV-1

HIV-1 is the type behind most infections worldwide. Its closest relatives are SIV found in chimpanzees (SIVcpz) and, for a few rare groups, in gorillas (SIVgor), in forests of Cameroon and neighbouring countries.

🌿

HIV-2

HIV-2 came from SIV in sooty mangabeys, a monkey of West Africa. It is less easily passed on and is found mostly in West Africa.

The jump from animal to human happened on several separate occasions, which is why HIV-1 has four groups (M, N, O and P). Only group M spread across the world. The others stayed mostly in Central Africa.

How did the virus cross over?

The most widely accepted explanation is the “hunter” theory. People hunted and butchered chimpanzees for meat, and a cut on the hand could let infected blood enter the person's body. Usually the body would fight off an animal virus like SIV. On a few rare occasions, the virus adapted to its new human host and became HIV.

When and where did the pandemic begin?

Genetic studies suggest that HIV-1 group M began spreading in humans around 1920 in Kinshasa, then part of the Belgian Congo (today the Democratic Republic of the Congo). The oldest HIV sample scientists have found comes from a blood sample taken in Kinshasa in 1959.

For decades the virus spread slowly. Researchers think a mix of factors then helped it take off: rapid city growth, new railways that moved large numbers of people, and big changes in society in the 1950s and 1960s. From Central Africa it later reached the rest of the world.

✳

Nobody is to blame. A virus crossing between species is a natural event, and it says nothing about any person, group or country today. Be wary of rumours that HIV was created on purpose or spread through a vaccine. Scientific evidence does not support these claims.

03 · Inside the body

What HIV does to the body

The immune system uses white blood cells called CD4 cells to fight infections. HIV enters these cells, makes copies of itself and destroys them. Over time, with no treatment, the body has fewer and fewer CD4 cells and can no longer fight off illnesses that a healthy body would handle easily.

AIDS (Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome) is the most advanced stage of HIV infection. HIV and AIDS are not the same thing. A person with HIV does not have AIDS, and with treatment most people never reach that stage.

04 · Effective care

Treatment works

The body cannot get rid of HIV, and there is currently no cure. Once a person has HIV, they have it for life. The good news is that effective treatment exists. It is a daily medicine called antiretroviral therapy (ART).

Taken as prescribed, ART lowers the amount of virus in the blood (the viral load) to a very low level. This is called viral suppression. When the level is so low that a standard lab test cannot detect it, the person has an undetectable viral load.

U = U

Undetectable = Untransmittable. People who take their HIV medicine as prescribed and keep an undetectable viral load can live long, healthy lives and cannot pass HIV to their partners through sex.

For more information, visit HIV.gov, the official website of the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services.

05 · Know the facts

How HIV is passed on

HIV spreads only through certain body fluids, and only in specific ways:

  • Sex without a condom or other protection
  • Sharing needles or syringes
  • From a mother to her baby during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding. With the right treatment, this risk is very low.
♡

HIV is not passed on by hugging, shaking hands, sharing food or cups, or sharing a toilet. Everyday contact is safe.

06 · Take care of yourself

Prevention and testing

HIV can be prevented. These are the main ways to protect yourself and others:

⌕

Test regularly

Testing is the only way to know your status. Early treatment protects your health and your partners.

♡

Use condoms

Use condoms correctly every time you have sex.

＋

Ask about PrEP

PrEP is a medicine that people who do not have HIV can take to lower their risk of getting it.

⏱

Know about PEP

PEP is emergency medicine taken after a possible exposure. It must be started as soon as possible, within 72 hours.

  • Never share needles or syringes.
  • Pregnancy and HIV: pregnant women with HIV who take treatment can greatly lower the chance of passing HIV to their baby.
＋

If you are worried about a possible exposure, visit a nearby clinic or health worker for advice urgently. PEP must be started within 72 hours, and sooner is better.

07 · A timeline

A short history

  1. c. 1920

    Early spread

    HIV-1 group M begins to spread among people in Kinshasa.

  2. 1981

    First recognised AIDS cases

    Doctors in the United States report the first recognised cases of what would later be called AIDS.

  3. 1983–86

    The virus is identified

    Scientists identify the virus that causes AIDS, and in 1986 it is named HIV.

  4. 1996

    Combination therapy

    Combination antiretroviral therapy becomes available, turning HIV from a usually fatal illness into a manageable condition.

  5. Today

    Living well with HIV

    People on effective treatment live long, healthy lives, and U=U shows that they cannot pass HIV on through sex when viral suppression is maintained.

08 · Science in progress

Latest research

HIV science is moving fast. These are the main developments reported in 2026, mostly at the AIDS 2026 conference. Many are still being tested and are not yet available as everyday care.

Prevention: an injection twice a year

Lenacapavir is an injection that protects against HIV for six months at a time. In the large PURPOSE 1 trial, 2 of 2,134 people taking it got HIV, compared with 77 of 3,204 people taking a daily pill. In follow-up studies, about 95% of participants chose to keep using the injection rather than go back to daily pills. People gave convenience, fewer pills and less stigma as reasons.

Other options are coming too. A once-monthly PrEP pill called MK-8527 is in late-stage trials, and the dapivirine vaginal ring is a monthly option that women control themselves.

Is lenacapavir available in Kenya?

Kenya's Pharmacy and Poisons Board approved it in January 2026, and the national rollout began on 26 February 2026. It is offered free at public health facilities, alongside daily oral PrEP and condoms.

The rollout is happening in three phases. The first phase covered 15 counties with the highest HIV burden, and coverage is meant to grow until it reaches all 47 counties. If you are outside the early counties, ask at your nearest public health facility whether it is available yet.

Not everyone qualifies. You must test negative for HIV and have had no possible exposure in the last 72 hours. Tell the health worker about any other medicine you take, including TB treatment, because some medicines can interact with it.

Sources: Kenya Ministry of Health, national rollout launch, Citizen Digital.

Treatment: from daily pills to weekly pills

Researchers are working to make treatment less of a daily burden. Detailed results from two trials of a once-a-week pill that combines islatravir and lenacapavir were presented in 2026. A weekly pill looks close, but it still needs final approval.

Towards a cure

Two more people were reported cured after stem-cell transplants. These transplants are risky and are only done when someone also has a serious blood cancer, so they cannot be offered to most people with HIV.

A more promising path for many people uses broadly neutralising antibodies. These are immune proteins that can recognise many kinds of HIV. In several studies, a good share of participants kept a low or undetectable viral load for a long time without taking daily medicine. This is called remission.

Vaccines

There is still no HIV vaccine. Scientists report steady progress in teaching the immune system to make the rare antibodies that protect against many kinds of HIV, including by giving carefully timed doses to steer the immune response in the right direction.

✳

Funding matters. A survey presented at a major HIV conference in early 2026 found that cuts to PEPFAR funding disrupted services at 47% of the clinics surveyed. New treatments only help when people can reach them.

None of this changes today's best advice. Test regularly, use proven prevention, and anyone with HIV should keep taking their medicine as prescribed. Research results can change quickly, so check with a health worker for what is available near you.

Sources: IAS, AIDS 2026 key takeaways, aidsmap, antibody studies and a cure, AJMC, CROI 2026 summary. Information current as of October 2026.

ⓘ

09 · Share the truth

Myths and facts

Misinformation can cause real harm. Here are a few common claims worth correcting.

✕ Myths

  • HIV is curable.
  • HIV is a curse.
  • You can get HIV from hugging or sharing a cup.
  • HIV and AIDS are the same thing.

✓ Facts

  • HIV can be prevented.
  • HIV can be managed with treatment.
  • HIV is a virus that began in animals and spread between people. It is not a curse.
  • Everyday contact is safe.
  • AIDS is the late stage of HIV, and treatment prevents it for most people.

Share knowledge. Lead with compassion.
